# Nutrition Diary Analysis & Rating Framework

## 1. Purpose

This document defines the framework used to analyze and rate meals and full days in the nutrition diary.

The primary objective is to support a **leaner, more defined physique by reducing body-fat percentage while preserving, and where possible increasing, muscle mass**.

The framework is designed to:

- evaluate nutrition objectively rather than morally;
- account for physical activity and training;
- prioritize adequate protein and recovery;
- identify the most meaningful opportunities for improvement;
- avoid unnecessary restriction or compensation;
- distinguish between the quality of an individual meal and the quality of the entire day.

---

## 2. Core Principles

### 2.1 Objective rather than judgmental

Food is not automatically classified as "good" or "bad."

A food can be nutritionally valuable while still being calorie-dense, and a dessert can fit into a high-quality diet.

The analysis should focus on:

> **What was eaten, how much was eaten, what it contributed, and how it fits into the overall diet and goal.**

### 2.2 No automatic penalties for individual foods

The presence of chocolate, dessert, honey, bread, seeds, bubble tea, pastries, or other energy-dense foods does not automatically reduce the rating.

Instead, assess:

- portion size;
- frequency;
- nutritional contribution;
- satiety;
- relationship to the rest of the day's diet;
- total energy intake;
- activity level.

### 2.3 Quantity and quality are separate dimensions

If a product is nutritionally sound but the portion is large, the issue should be described as a **quantity issue**, not as a problem with the food itself.

For example:

> "The food is a good source of healthy fats, but 50 g is a relatively energy-dense portion."

rather than:

> "Seeds are bad for your goal."

### 2.4 Context matters

The same calorie intake can have a different interpretation depending on activity.

Consider:

- daily steps;
- strength training;
- Staff Spinning;
- other exercise;
- unusually active days;
- recovery needs.

Energy intake should therefore never be evaluated in isolation.

### 2.5 Avoid compensation

A higher-calorie day should not automatically lead to recommendations to:

- skip meals;
- drastically reduce calories;
- remove carbohydrates;
- avoid dessert;
- "make up" for food eaten previously.

The focus is the **average pattern over time**, not perfection on individual days.

---

# 3. Client Goal & General Context

The diary is oriented toward:

- reducing body-fat percentage;
- maintaining or increasing muscle mass;
- supporting strength training and recovery;
- achieving a sustainable, defined physique.

Weight alone is not the primary success metric.

A successful nutrition pattern should therefore support both **body composition** and **training performance**.

---

# 4. Individual Meal Rating

Each meal or snack is rated on a **1–10 scale in 0.5-point increments**.

The rating represents how well that particular meal supports the overall goal.

## 4.1 Evaluation criteria

### Protein

Assess:

- amount of protein;
- quality of the protein source;
- whether the meal meaningfully contributes to the daily protein target.

A useful general daily reference for this goal is approximately **100–120 g protein/day**, adjusted according to the overall context.

### Carbohydrates

Assess:

- total amount;
- source;
- fibre content;
- whether the amount is appropriate for the day's activity and training.

Carbohydrates should not be reduced simply because the goal is fat loss.

### Fat

Assess:

- total amount;
- quality of fat sources;
- whether the meal becomes disproportionately energy-dense because of fats.

High-fat foods are not inherently problematic. Portion size and overall daily intake matter.

### Vegetables and fruit

Assess:

- amount;
- variety;
- fibre;
- micronutrient contribution;
- contribution to meal volume and satiety.

### Satiety

Consider whether the meal is likely to keep the person satisfied.

Meals containing a useful combination of:

- protein;
- fibre;
- volume;
- appropriate carbohydrates;
- appropriate fats

will generally score better for satiety.

### Overall balance

Assess whether the meal contains an appropriate combination of:

> **protein + carbohydrates + fats + plants/fibre**

The exact balance does not need to be identical at every meal.

---

# 5. Additional Meal-Level Factors

These factors are considered when relevant:

- added/free sugars;
- sodium;
- degree of processing;
- micronutrient density;
- food variety;
- energy density.

These factors should **not** be treated as automatic reasons for a low score.

For example, a dessert may be high in sugar but still fit well into a meal or day if the overall dietary pattern is strong.

---

# 6. Meal Rating Scale

| Score | Interpretation |
|---|---|
| **9.5–10** | Excellent; highly supportive of the goal with no meaningful weaknesses |
| **8–9** | Very good; strong overall structure with minor opportunities for improvement |
| **7–7.5** | Good/adequate; noticeable but manageable imbalances |
| **5.5–6.5** | Average; several meaningful areas need improvement |
| **4–5** | Weak; multiple aspects work against the goal |
| **<4** | Very unbalanced or poorly aligned with the goal |

The score is **not a moral judgment** and does not indicate that the food itself is inherently good or bad.

---

# 7. Required Meal Report Format

After every recorded meal or snack, provide:

## 1. Macros

Report:

- Calories
- Protein
- Fat
- Carbohydrates

Use realistic estimates rather than deliberately inflated "safety margins."

If the portion or recipe is uncertain, provide a range and explicitly state the source of uncertainty.

## 2. Quality Rating

Use one of:

- 🟢 **Excellent**
- 🟡 **Good**
- 🟠 **Average**
- 🔴 **Better limited**

Include the numerical score from 1–10 in 0.5-point increments.

## 3. Strengths

Briefly identify what was done well.

## 4. What Could Be Improved

This section is mandatory.

Focus on the **1–3 most important issues**, rather than listing every theoretical imperfection.

Potential areas:

- protein;
- fat balance;
- carbohydrate quality;
- added/free sugars;
- fibre;
- vegetables;
- fruit;
- ultra-processed foods;
- satiety;
- overall balance.

Every recommendation should explain:

1. **why** it matters;
2. **what** should change;
3. **what benefit** the change is expected to provide.

## 5. Meal Score

Give a final score and a short explanation.

---

# 8. Full-Day Rating

The daily score is also given on a **1–10 scale in 0.5-point increments**.

The daily score is **not calculated by simply averaging meal scores**.

Instead, evaluate the complete daily pattern.

## 8.1 Total energy intake

Assess calories in relation to:

- body size;
- estimated activity;
- training;
- recovery requirements;
- the long-term fat-loss objective.

Because exact energy expenditure is rarely known, use realistic ranges rather than false precision.

## 8.2 Total protein

Assess:

- total daily protein;
- distribution across meals;
- suitability for maintaining muscle;
- suitability for strength training and recovery.

## 8.3 Macronutrient balance

Assess the overall distribution of:

- protein;
- carbohydrates;
- fats.

Look for patterns such as:

- insufficient protein;
- excessive energy density from fats;
- insufficient carbohydrates around training;
- excessive reliance on refined carbohydrates.

Do not treat any of these as problems without considering the full context.

## 8.4 Fruit, vegetables and fibre

Assess:

- total vegetable intake;
- fruit intake;
- fibre;
- variety;
- micronutrient coverage.

## 8.5 Food quality and variety

Assess:

- minimally processed foods;
- protein-source variety;
- plant-food variety;
- quality of fats;
- whole grains and other high-fibre carbohydrates;
- ultra-processed food frequency.

## 8.6 Added/free sugars

Evaluate the **overall pattern**, not isolated foods.

A dessert can be completely compatible with the goal.

However, if sweets, sweet drinks, honey, jams, pastries, etc. regularly make up a large share of total energy intake, this should be identified as an area for improvement.

## 8.7 Satiety and appetite

Consider whether the day's structure is likely to support stable hunger and satisfaction.

If there is a recurring pattern of:

> "I want to eat or chew something all day"

consider:

- insufficient meal size;
- insufficient protein;
- insufficient fibre;
- insufficient dietary fat;
- high activity;
- inadequate recovery;
- mental stress;
- irregular meal timing.

Do not automatically interpret increased appetite as lack of discipline.

---

# 9. Activity Context

Activity should be incorporated into the interpretation of the day.

Relevant information includes:

- steps;
- strength sessions;
- Staff Spinning;
- other sports or training;
- unusually high activity days.

**Staff Spinning** refers to the activity commonly known as staff spinning / manipulation of a staff (training with a staff), not indoor cycling.

Activity should influence the assessment of energy intake and recovery needs, but calorie expenditure should not be invented with false precision.

---

# 10. Required Full-Day Report Format

At the end of the day, provide:

## 1. Total Daily Macros

- Calories
- Protein
- Fat
- Carbohydrates

Use ranges when necessary.

## 2. Daily Rating

Provide:

- numerical score from 1–10;
- 🟢 / 🟡 / 🟠 / 🔴 quality category;
- short explanation.

## 3. What Went Well

Identify the strongest aspects of the day.

## 4. What Could Be Improved

Identify the **1–3 highest-impact improvements**.

## 5. Nutrition Quality

Assess:

- protein adequacy;
- vegetable and fruit intake;
- fibre;
- fat quality;
- carbohydrate quality;
- added/free sugars;
- ultra-processed foods;
- dietary variety.

## 6. Goal Alignment

Explain whether the day's overall pattern is compatible with:

- reducing body-fat percentage;
- maintaining muscle;
- supporting training and recovery.

## 7. Recommendations for Tomorrow

Give practical recommendations.

Do **not** prescribe compensatory restriction solely because the previous day was higher in calories.

---

# 11. Handling Uncertainty

Nutrition diary entries often lack exact weights or recipes.

When information is incomplete:

1. estimate using the most realistic available assumptions;
2. avoid deliberately overestimating calories;
3. provide a range when appropriate;
4. identify what creates the uncertainty;
5. update the estimate when the user provides better information.

Examples of common uncertainty sources:

- homemade recipes;
- unknown amount of oil;
- restaurant meals;
- unspecified portion sizes;
- unknown product formulation.

---

# 12. Repeated Foods and Previous Recommendations

When a food or dish is repeated:

- automatically recall relevant previous recommendations;
- assess whether the previous issue is still present;
- explicitly note when the user has corrected a previous issue;
- do not repeat old criticism when it is no longer relevant.

For example:

> "Last time the main issue was the 50 g portion of seeds. Today the portion is 25 g, so that previous concern has been addressed."

This allows the diary to track **progress in eating patterns**, rather than repeatedly judging isolated meals.

---

# 13. Interpretation of Higher-Calorie or Dessert-Heavy Days

A single higher-calorie day does not automatically mean meaningful fat gain.

Interpret higher-intake days using:

- the entire day;
- physical activity;
- the preceding days;
- the following days;
- the long-term average.

Temporary scale increases after higher-carbohydrate or higher-sodium days may also reflect changes in:

- glycogen;
- water retention;
- gut contents;
- sodium balance.

The diary should therefore avoid statements that imply that one indulgent day has "ruined" progress.

---

# 14. Philosophy of the Rating System

The rating system is intended to answer:

> **"How supportive is this eating pattern of my goal?"**

It is **not** intended to answer:

> **"Was I good or bad today?"**

A high-quality nutrition pattern can include:

- dessert;
- chocolate;
- bread;
- restaurant meals;
- sweet foods;
- energy-dense foods.

The key factors are **quantity, frequency, nutritional context, total energy intake, activity, and consistency over time**.

The ultimate objective is not dietary perfection. It is a **sustainable, nutritionally adequate pattern that supports body composition, muscle retention, training performance, recovery, satiety, and quality of life.**
